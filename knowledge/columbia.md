Brand Traits to Highlight for Columbia:
While all schools are looking for leadership / evidence of a stellar career so far (to thus lead to a stellar career going forward), a personal quality that CBS looks for more than other schools is the ability to pick yourself up again after facing a set-back. 
Honestly guys, I've attended OVER EIGHT yearly admissions consultants' conferences, and if I had a nickle for each time their (lovely) senior AdCom member Michael Robinson uses the word "resilience" as something he loves to see... well, I wouldn't be updating this advice right now and would instead be on a tropical beach somewhere, ha ha ha...!  (seriously though: see examples below)
They're also looking for people who are going to actively make the most of their awesome NYC location (and thus launch themselves into awesome careers as a result). They do NOT want people who will just sit at home and wait for a job to land in their lap. If there's ANYPLACE on earth where you can hustle, network, and make stuff happen... it's NYC. So showing that level of resourcefulness, not taking "no" for an answer but trying again and again.
NOW. They also make a lot of noise in their marketing about themselves about looking for “innovative / entrepreneurial” people. If you’re super-innovative, then try to bring evidence of this in (e.g. probably in essay 1, when using past evidence to prove that you’ve got what it takes to reach your career goal), but personally I’ve seen evidence of tenacity (not to mention, more conventional career paths) get more love from them. I can’t help but wonder if they worry that someone who is waaaaay too innovative might secretly be hoping to get into MIT / GSB / etc. instead. 
However, at the 2023 conference, the school's relatively-newish Dean (he got the job in 2019) identified Sustainability / Renewable Energy; Digital / AI; and biotech as some high-potential trends he sees... he is moving curricular offerings in that direction, so we can assume that HIS values will start to trickle down to what Admissions looks for! 
The Lab traits that most closely match to these values are:
1. Determined (something they value re: your hustle in leveraging NYC location to get a cool internship / project!) 
2. Overcoming Obstacles
3.Persuasive, if persuasion was needed to convince someone to, e.g. give you / your ideas a shot
4. Innovative, but primarily if your career vision is techy, and even then I wouldn't over-sell it lest they worry that they're not your first choice. 
I can vouch for the "tenacity" piece in three (as of May 2023, now SIX!) ways:
1. Several years ago, Michael Robinson -- now the Senior Director of admissions -- state in a webinar that he likes the stories of people who "don't quit / don't let a setback define them"
2. I was in a small group discussion at an admissions consulting conference (in-person, pre-pandemic) and I was in the same group with Michael and he again specifically cited how much he admires people who have "overcome obstacles"
3. A former ApplicantLab client who got in a couple years ago made sure to mention his tribulations trying to find a job after a layoff (as "supporting evidence" in a former essay topic, describing something others would be "pleasantly surprised" to learn about him), instead of trying to sweep that period of unemployment under the rug. He was accepted, so that honesty did not hurt him... I think in part since it actually gave him an opportunity to spot-light his tenacity !
4. In fact,  a year or two after THAT happened, at an AIGAC admissions consultants' conference I attended, one of the then-senior AdCom members (Amanda Carlso) admitted that SHE had been laid off earlier in her career, during the first "dot com bubble burst".... so that she empathized with people who had dusted themselves off after a setback and gotten back on the proverbial horse! So they "walk the talk" in loving tenacity stories! 
5. In May 2022, again at the AIGAC conference, Michael Robinson *again* mentioned that he admires people who have resilience.
- He specifically added they look for this since it's important for someone's career vision as well -- how can you prove to them that you'll be able to "stay motivated" in solving a specific problem over the course of 10+ years? 
6. Yoo-hoo! It's 2023, I just got back from THIS year's AIGAC conference, and I think I heard Michael use the word "resilience" maybe 3 times when, e.g., gushing about a student they had let in that he loved, or a current student showing tenacity in their job hunt, etc... !

What about the January Entry term ("J-term): The "J-Term" starts in January and thus does not have a summer internship. 
J-Term is ideal for:
- Older candidates (who can claim that they want to continue to "move up" in their existing career -- EVEN IF once they GET there, they hustle like crazy during that first term to get a school-year internship)
- Younger candidates who have family businesses to go back to (ie, "Mommy will hire me and pay me a lot, so don't worry about me repaying the loans!") 
- Entrepreneurs who, at least, CLAIM they will go back to their existing business (but of course can also hustle during the school year to get an internship and try to make a career switch with it) 
- Note: in order for this story to work, the business needs at least SOME solid level of revenue (or POTENTIAL revenue) in order to justify the $200k price tag of the degree... 
Now, the IDEA behind J-term is that you can't switch careers with it...but I HAVE had a client in the past successfully use J-Term to switch careers; he just hustled like crazy to do term-time internships during the school year in lieu of a summer internship and it all worked out. HOWEVER, he did NOT APPLY with this story!!!! This is not the INTENTION for the J-Term program -- so if you are planning on doing this, do not TELL them that in the essay since they'll view you as risky!"So why would I apply to J-term, then?" 
Well... because the chances of getting in are way, way higher. And you'll spend the 2nd year with the "regular" class, thus meeting those folks, building connections, etc. 
Also -- to be blunt -- getting in via J-Term is WAY EASIER! On a podcast that I recorded with John Byrne (founder of Poets and Quants), he gave numbers that would indicate an essentially 50% admissions rate for J-TERM?  Whoa.  That sounds crazy to me... (is it possible that # isn't accurate???)  but I ran with that number and here's how it looks, in a nice chart form:

 
HOWEVER -- do NOT apply to J-term unless you are ready and willing to enroll this upcoming January, AND you are totally fine WITHOUT having a summer internship. 
While I have had clients in the past navigate a career switch via J-term (e.g. via projects  / mini internships during the school year), it's certainly going to be much harder vs. the traditional summer internship. 
- Also note: If you are applying with a "career switch story", it might be HARDER to get in via J-term if it seems that you'd need that summer internship!!! Don't think that they won't notice the absence of it, and thus the proposed path for the career might get extra scrutiny! 
Also, I recently had a Lab user reach out with the resourceful and understandable idea to perhaps apply to J-term, use it as a "back up", and then apply to R1 schools and see if they get in elsewhere. THE PROBLEM WITH THIS STRATEGY is that... R1 decisions come out in early or mid-Dec.... (assuming that you get accepted / rejected, vs. the waitlist!) 
... if you get accepted via J-term, you need to pay a $2,000 deposit immediately and $1,000 by end of October, you need to start getting sudent loans and perhaps a student visa immediately, you'll need to find a place to live in NYC *prior* to classes starting in January.... and I'm guessing you'd also need to pay the first major tuition payment prior to January. 
...In other words, just in case the other R1 schools don't work out, you'd potentially be on the hook for a minimum of $3,000, and possibly much, much more!That might be an expensive gamble indeed, so only take it if you're comfortable being in that position! 
If you then end up getting in off of a waitlist somewhere else, I guess technically you could drop out of CBS to enroll in the other school in August, but... surely by THAT point you'd already have had to pay a much larger amount for the first chunk of classes? Plus the other school might feel icky if they find out you did this... icky enough to rescind admission? Not definitely, but... I could certainly see it happening?

Immediate post-MBA goal
Please don't over-think this one. Thankfully, you'll get a chance to explain things more in-depth in Essay #1 (especially useful for career changers) and/or even in the shorter "Why CBS / Why NYC?" essay (currently known as essay #3).
The examples that THEY give have been the same for a few years now, so we can guess that they like them: 
“Work in business development for a media company.”
“Join a strategy consulting firm.”
“Launch a data-management start-up.”
So honestly, don't over-think this, BUT if you are looking for some advice, any advice, to get you started, here goes:
I think the best way to start this (in case you're stuck) is to start with a "To" verb, to make it immediately clear if you plan to PROCEED in your current path, TRANSITION in a big way, or make more of a LATERAL move (either your industry or function will the same).
Examples:
"To apply my IT skills as Product Manager."
"To advance further in film finance."
"To transition to an emerging-markets hedge fund." 
"To adapt my mortgage lending background to a REIT."
If there isn't enough room to get into the whole "how my background ties into this" thing, don't worry -- it's fine with this space to keep things simple, because you can make the case more solidly in the "Career Vision / Why Columbia?" essay. What's funny is that in the past, this blurb had a longer limit (75 characters), and now it's been reduced... but the word-count for the "Why Columbia" and "Why New York?" essays have gone up! So the trade-off is one I'm happy to make!
Realize that is probably one of the first "real" pieces of content they will read in your application, alongside your resume, so if you're applying with a career change story, realize that right off the bat, the reader might be going into Essay #1 with just a bit of skepticism. The good news is, if you know of a realistic *summer internship* you COULD get which is a viable path to the post-MBA goal, then now you have the opportunity to show that you've done your homework and thought this through! 
 
WARNING WARNING: Do NOT say that you simply want to, "Get a job in consulting" or "Attain an Associate position at an Investment Bank." Way too cliched. At least try to be specific (What TYPE of consulting? Which FOCUS in finance?) and see if you can include any specifics from what you currently do, ie: "To utilize my background to analyze M&A deals in the telecom space."
See the "Career Vision" section to avoid common "I want to be a consultant/banker" pitfalls. 

"Why J-Term?"
Why do you prefer the January-entry term? (50 characters maximum)
This is where you make it CLEAR that you WON'T be asking them later to move you to the August entry co-hort (which they won't allow), you won't whine to them later because the lack of summer internship is hindering your career plans, etc. 
This one is, thankfully, a relatively easy one: 
- To get back to my previous employer / my startup / my family business ASAP 
- This is, candidly, the biggest reason anyone should have for preferring J-term? Obviously you'd word it in your own way, but the fact that you have something else to GO BACK TO and thus do NOT need the internship part of the typical 2-year program
- "But Maria I actually secretly need the summer internship for my career goals" -- then either this actually isn't the program for you, OR... welp, COME UP WITH A DIFFERENT CAREER PLAN, one in which you DON'T need an internship
-In a pinch, can you ask your current employer: "Hey, if I were to go get an MBA, could I theoretically be hired back, but in a _____ role instead?"  and even if in real life this is the last thing on earth you want to do, sorry kiddo, but it's what you've got to say, to avoid their fears that you'll freak out later on. 
- "Ok so I understand why sponsored applicants might prefer this to the full 2-year option, but why would anyone else apply to this?"
- Because, to be blunt, CBS J-term is frankly the best shot that "young" (3 years or less) or "old" (8 years or more) have at gettnig into an M7 school.  Because J-term is intended for folks who either have something to go back to (or can plausibly succesfully engage in full-time recruiting the 2nd year, despite the lack of internship), it is bluntly easier to get in to J-term vs. the "regular" way. 
- But do NOT apply to it unless you are READY to withdraw applications to all other schools and move to NYC should you get accepted. Also, scholarship money can often be lower for J-term, so if you woud be dependent upon getting a scholarship to go, then don't apply (in fairness to them, strong candidates either have a job to go back to, OR a family business that makes enough $$ such that the family can easily absorb the cost of the program, so I don't blame them for giving out less $$!)

What you put here should be a "realistic bridge" from where you are NOW to where you want to go (in the OTHER 50-character answer about post-MBA goals) 
- If you're a career switcher: is there an "intermediate" step between "now" and "post-MBA" that will help set you up for that post-MBA job?

- If you're a SPONSORED candidate who will be returning back to your current employer, what's an experience you wouldn't otherwise be able to easily get once you're back at the current mothership... that will HELP you in the post-MBA role? 
- Obviously if you're applying with a "Startup" vision, then you MUST say that you'll spend the summer working on your venture! Otherwise, your commitment to your startup idea will be questioned? 
To me, what's more important than the "WHAT" here is the "WHY" this is the choice you are making and/or proving to me that you're realistic and have done homework - if you're struggling to fit in the 50 characters, you can probably remove the word "intership", since it's implied? :
- Sub-optimal: "Internship in consulting, either MBB or Deloitte." (49 chars)
- Better: "Consulting internship; my research says it's vital"  (50 chars)
 ^^^ Shows that you've done homework into that career path vs. simply assuming that you'd be handed an MBB job post-MBA
- Sub-optimal: "PM intership at a large tech company" (too vague?) 
- Better: "PM in FinTech as intro to regulations"
 - ^^^ More specific; tells me WHY this internship makes sense

Essay 2: Please share a specific example of how you made a team more collaborative, more inclusive or fostered a greater sense of community within an organization. (250 words)
In a hurry? Suggested outline for this essay 
The events described in this essay MUST have a clear "before" and "after"... in the middle, you'll describe YOUR SPECIFIC ACTIONS. Not what "we" did ("WE" are not applying)... what YOU did. 
- What was the "before" / "status quo" situation you faced? If not obvious (which it very well might be!) why was the status quo sub-optimal 
- That is, if it's not obvious by the way you describe it, WHAT about this circumstance was originally less collaborative, less inclusive, or an organization that lacked a sense of community? 
- This could be an assumption about others that you encountered (a bias); a conflict / mis-communication between groups; a prevailing way of doing things (maybe even a policy) at work or school. "We don't have time to care about culture here; there's too much work to do!", etc.
- What specific thing(s) did you do to try to mitigate / improve this? 
- Must be YOUR actions, not "as a team, WE decided to..." -- the "team" is not applying to business school, you are.
- If applicable, was there an emotional insight that hit you? 
- One that pertained to YOU: Learning about systemic inequality in the news, a movie, a book; discovering that your friend is undocumented; meeting a gay couple across the hall that are amazing dads; working with someone who told you their story and/or who blew your biases to smithereens?
- One that pertained to OTHERS: Realizing that your boss / company's policy was (perhaps unintiontally.. or perhaps intentionally) unfairly excluding people for some b.s. reason; figuring out what it would take to open someone's eyes to their biases (or to overcome them); realizing that others didn't even realize there was a problem to begin with?; figuring out why two groups just couldn't seem to get on the same wavelength? 
- Something that stemmed from your own experience? "Hearing my mother talk about how she wasn't allowed to go to school as a child..."; "As the sole AAPI employee in my team, when the hate crimes against East Asians broke out last year, I..."; "When my sister came out as transgender last year, I realized just how little I knew about trans issues...", "When I tranferred to a new high school, it was really tough to make new friends. That's why when my company asked for help in making new hires feel more welcome, I knew I had to volunteer for the on-boarding social committee..." 
- So, uh...what happened? What was the result / impact? 
- Unlike typical leadership essays, where being able to point to some sort of concrete triumph is necessary... here, even budging the bigotry boulder a little bit is better than nothing. Making a snobby team nicer to that new guy might not have changed the world, but it's better than nothing. Getting more people to show up to your department's monthly happy hour isn't gonna win the Nobel Peace Prize (sorry), it's better than nothing. While sure, it would be awesome if you were able to change a hiring policy etc...even just changing some "hearts and minds" for a situation like this is better than nothing. 
- Note: the question text specifically asks for *A* time that you did this. Therefore, your essay should deal primarily with ONE core example. However, don't forget that you can use the concept of "supporting evidence" as a pre-cursor or post-script to The Main Story -- not only as a sort of "origin story" for how you decided to get involved, but could also be a type of "...and after this, I later went on to [quick description of some other thing you did that was nice but not worthy of being the main story here]

More detail: 
For the "Why NYC?" component, a common mistake here is to write an over-the-top love letter to NYC ("I love New York's energy and great pizza! Fuggedaboutit!"), but if it’s a letter that just about anyone else could write, then it’s not good enough. You need to tie NYC to your career plans (industry, internships, networking / proximity to speakers & industry leaders), heck, even your passions and personality.
Video overview of some "Why NYC?" arguments -- with written explanation underneath. If you're in a hurry, skip the video and just skim the points below to see which ones most strongly match with supporting your future career goals:
Here are some good “Why NYC?” arguments:
Your future industry is centered in NYC (finance, media, advertising... MANY fields are “centered” there...) the good news is, SO many industries have “hubs” in NYC, so going there for school makes sense if you want to develop a strong network there, get internships, etc. It especially makes sense if you want to LIVE in NYC after graduation!  
If you are pursuing a traditional job search: the fact that the NYC is the location for either the headquarters or large offices of big employers can be a big benefit.
If you are pursuing a non-traditional "networked" job search: so much the better! If several companies / organizations in your target field are in NYC, then you'll ESPECIALLY need that proximity to them to be able to meet with them.
Note! I recently watched a webinar that CBS's career center did, and they said that more than half of job offers are made informally, that is, NOT via "on-campus recruiting" but via more informal routes, which I have to assume means networking! 
Your future profession will be one in which you’ll do business often with NYC, such that spending 2 years building your network (while getting your MBA) will be super-useful. Which sorts of people could help you later in life, even if you don’t plan to live in NYC? 
For example, if you want to keep working in entertainment, or tech, or banking in your home country, could you make a point along the lines of, “I’ve spent the past several years developing a strong network in [current city]. If I can expand my network to NYC, I’ll be able to use that network for the rest of my life, since I can [What? Get more investors? Expand your company’s presence to the US? Now have “more bases covered” in terms of cities where you have contacts, and therefore will be able to accomplish more?]” Can you learn best practices from the people you’ll meet in NYC?
New York is bound to have many "meet ups" and organizations that are not necessarily tied to the school. While your first choice should always be to mention an on-campus club, if what you are interested in is VERY niche, can you research and find an organization in the city that is focused on your area of interest?
Future entrepreneurs: NYC’s VC / start-up scene is fast growing -- can you claim to want to tap it, OR could your future startup sell in to big companies based in NYC?
 
A key way to network will be via the CBS-run conferences. Since these conferences can be SUCH a great way to meet people, and since the conferences usually happen in NYC, you can mention your contribution to organizing the conference (and therefore the executives you will meet by doing that) in this essay. 
MOST of the Conferences are run by the student clubs. In case you need that link to the clubs again, here you go: https://www8.gsb.columbia.edu/programs/mba/student-life/student-organizations
Example: "By volunteering to help organize the Marketing Association of Columbia's annual conference, I'll get to directly network with executives in the cosmetics industry. Last year's conference had speakers from NYC-based companies such as Glossier and Soko Glam; CBS's location will make it easier to find executives from other NYC-based beauty companies as well."

A list of them is here (and remember, don't simply say" I want to meet with one!" or "I want to meet with Jane Doe!", but point out which specific facet of that person's experience will be useful for you, and also WHAT you'd want to do with your precious, limited time with them: Have them review your business plan? Term-time internship - I think everyone should mention this: NYC’s wealth of companies and Columbia’s proximity to them makes getting a internship much easier (especially one during the school year!)-- which can help secure a post-MBA job.
This isn’t a trivial point; when I explored a career switch during b-school (for which I had ZERO experience), I was told that with no experience, I was screwed. Well, HBS’s location in Boston, a city without a ton of companies in that field, its unenthusiastic view on taking term-time internships, and the fact that I didn’t have a car so I couldn't have even GOTTEN to the internship had I secured one -- definitely put me at a disadvantage vs. people in, say, NYC/LA/SF. Career-switchers in particular, take note!
You could even start working part-time during your last semester on your post-MBA job! AMAZING. (I have a friend who started part-time work during that last semester which turned into a full-time job after!)
 
If you grew up in a bustling urban environment, can you say that you miss it and want to be someplace similar again / continue to be in an environment like that? Bonus points: if you’ve previously thrown yourself into life in an urban environment with ZEST and energy, you can say you plan to mirror that experience in NYC.
In the video, they cite their world-class faculty, which is great -- you can go ahead and mention a specific rock-star professor if you want, but -- honestly, every top school has a great faculty. What NYC schools *DO* have that is unique is the potential to bring in MANY more high-powered speakers, since so many high-powered people find themselves in NYC at least once per year.
Not to keep comparing to HBS, but frankly we had a tougher time getting folks to speak on-campus / at conferences since frankly, Boston would be out of the way for them (vs. a mere afternoon detour during an already-planned trip). The speakers Columbia gets (and therefore the chance to maybe even MEET those people) is AMAZING.
Similarly, since so many accomplished people LIVE in NYC, many of them teach evening classes as “adjunct professors” (I have a friend who does this, in fact! He teaches a course in distressed debt at night -- something he wouldn't be able to do for a school located anywhere else)
 
Conversely, if you’ve never spent much time in any big city, much less NYC, can you say that you are thirsty to have such a dramatic change of pace?
Either way, just about anyone can cite that the energy of NYC is awesome, and you can think of no better place to study [business in general, or some specific aspect of business] than NYC.
However, hit this point lightly, if at all  -- SO many people can say this, so this isn't exactly going to help you stand out.
 
Finally, personal reasons -- I ALSO advise hitting this lightly, if at all (note that this might now be impossible given that OTHER "Why Columbia?" reasons now need to fit in!). But if you have a strong personal reason for being in NYC, then you can mention this -- but near the end, and not making a big deal of it:
"Finally, as a life-long theater buff, the thought of being able to unwind by taking in off-Broadway shows during the weekends is just the icing on the cake" ← don't say something like this unless you really have a strong history of supporting this - and can point to a concrete history of supporting the arts!! 
"Last but not least, my husband is about to graduate with his journalism degree and is looking to relocate to NYC. Columbia will allow us both to pursue our dreams simultaneously."
