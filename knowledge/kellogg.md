Random side-note: their recommendation letter has an extra question!
As you'll see when you get to the Recommendation module, an increasing number of schools use the "Common Recommendation" letter, administered by GMAC. 
The form has a number of check-boxes where your recommender will rate you, it asks in what capacity / how long your recommender has known you, etc. 
It also -- usually! -- asks for the response to 3 questions: 
How does the candidate’s performance compare to those of other well-qualified individuals in similar roles? Please provide specific examples. (300 words)
Describe the most important piece of constructive feedback you have given the candidate. Please detail the circumstances and the applicant’s response. (250 words)
(Optional) Is there anything else you would like us to know?
Well, Kellogg has those same questions, but they also added one of their own. It reads: 
Kellogg has a diverse student body and values students who are inclusive and encouraging of others with differing perspectives and backgrounds. Please tell us about a time when you witnessed the candidate living these values. (300 words)
If anything, this only re-iterates the importance to them of finding people who are able to bring different types of people into a conversation. Be aware that you'll need two stories (one for each recommender) that provide evidence of you living these values.

In a hurry?
- The easiest way to tackle the awkwardness of the essay plus the 2 career plan text boxes being in different sections of the application will be to start by sketching / drafting out a full  answer to essay 1, as if it were a “statement of purpose + a mini leadership story or two” essay (more on this later)...
- THEN, you can PULL out of that the stuff that will go into the 500-character (approximately 80-90 words) career text boxes that are within the application.
- This will be: the plan A short-term goal, the plan B short-term goal, the “functional” electives, the LT / people-focused / strategy electives.
- The newer version of this essay, with its additional 100 words (but, when we consider that a lot of the “Why Kellogg” specifics will now be moved to the career plan text boxes, I think you’ll net out at ~150 extra words total) should be, IMHO, primarily re-allocated to conveying a (brief!) version of a leadership story that shows you having Kellogg DNA.

Main essay elements
The main essay will then consist of:
- The Career Vision Hook (make sure to include some info as to how it evolved)
- “Here’s an overview of my career path so far”...to establish credibility...
- PLUS WITHIN THAT including a quick version of a leadership story and/or maybe a few referenced quickly, “Supporting Evidence” style, with the angle being about your development as a leader and how this has led you to the conclusion that, to continue to grow / improve your ability to make an impact, you need an MBA
- Some references to how the MBA will help you get there – this year, the two 500-character text boxes take away some of the “Why Kellogg specifics” heavy-lifting
- What CAN’T be outsourced to career boxes is your CONTRIBUTION to Kellogg, mostly likely via a professional club, but read on for a bunch of other ideas for how you can contribute

Academics:
Kellogg really values its student-driven culture AND its flexibility; lots of people major in 2+ things and/or do a "career focused pathway"and can really tailor their study across many electives – for another school’s application, this might make you look scattered; for Kellogg, it makes you seem ready to dive in.
Majors: many students pick 3 majors! Sometimes, groups of electives from different majors are combined together to make “pathways” which are unofficial roadmaps for ways to tailor the program to suit your particular needs:
http://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/programs/full-time-mba/academics/majors-pathways.aspx
If there is really one major that speaks to you, but you want to mention one more to prove your enthusiasm for Kellogg, pick "Managing Organizations", since it's the Swiss Army Knife major that can really apply to any scenario. Surely a few electives in that major will be useful for you!
Pick about 2 electives in that field of study (or 1 elective each, if you pick 2 - 3 majors). Don't forget to then explain a specific future scenario where you'll be putting that course's lessons into action.
As a rule of thumb, try to pick at least 1 course that will directly tie into helping you be successful in your post-MBA job (these are usually very function-specific courses), and at least 1 that will be useful as you climb up the leadership ranks (this will usually be a general managment course, or it could also be an experiential opportunity outside of the classroom (more below):
To save time, first click on the names of the major / pathways from the previous step -- those will take you to a site that lays out exactly what the courses are and sometimes also mentions "experiential" electives or opportunities in each major or pathway. 
There is also an opportunity to do Independent Studies with a professor; if you have a very specific career focus, it might be worth researching the professors to see if any of them have published recent research on this topic.

In a hurry?
MOST dual degrees (mercifully) generally ask a "statement of purpose" type of question -- e.g. "Career Goals? Why this degree, why our program?", so the good news is, the work / rationale / explanation building muscles you've been strengthening in the Lab will work here too...(just with different classes, etc., cited)
...you just need to balance between a career goal that TRULY REQUIRES both degrees (vs. "Well, why get a JD when you could just hire a lawyer later for a lot less money?") and also balance your ENTHUSIASM for and REFERENCES TO the academics / extra-curriculars / internship opportunities/  etc. between the 2 programs -- no lop-sided enthusiasm (TRUST ME: IT SHOWS)
Obviously, since the 2 applications will probably be read together, the career vision "hook" must be THE SAME in both essays (in terms of substance, NOT a copy-and-paste!), but you can obvious focus a bit more on the business side of things in the MBA essay and the (other degree) side of things in the (other degree) essay. 
As with any other "statement of purpose", you also need to establish your credibilty as someone who can make that career visiona actually happen -- what are your "bona fides"  / concrete experiences across BOTH areas? 
- If you don't have ANY concrete experience in one of the two degrees, then I think you should INSTEAD mention here the "homework" you've done to research: "Ok, do I actually NEED this other degree?" or perhaps more importantly, "How do I know I'll even ENJOY public policy / education / law / engineering / etc.?" 
- This could look something like shadowing someone in that role for a week, or speaking to lots of people who also did a dual-degree or a degree in the "other" field you don't yet have experience in.
As always, mention:
- Specific classes + a plausible future scenario where you'd really be putting that class' lessons to work 
- One sort of "Experiential" activity (if applicable) and not only "Hey, I want to go to Israel" but tell me WHY *that* particular trek / trip / project is useful for you
- At least one "club" or non-academic activity you plan to join, as well as what you can contribute to your fellow classmates.

Optional essay: We know that life is full of extenuating circumstances. Whether you want to explain gaps in work experience, your choice of recommenders, inconsistent or questionable academic performance or something else, you can use this section to briefly tell us anything we need to know about your application. (280 words)


[The advice for the optional essay is basically the same for all schools, so if you've read this for another school, it will be the same here!]
First of all, if you did have a major life-changing event that led to poor academics / periods of unemployment, consider weaving it into a main essay story, to address it head-on. If you do that, then you can potentially leave the optional essay blank. However, many schools have "Career Vision / Why MBA?" types of essays only, so in that case, there's a strong chance you'll need to write an optional essay too.
No matter why you're writing this, keep all optional essays:
- Brief
- Factual
- Not whiny ("Poor me / boo hoo!")
- Not too apologetic ("I totally suck! You'll probably reject me!")
- Mature. YES: MATURE. If you made a mistake, own up to it and what you learned from it. :)
- If applicable, use what I call the "silver lining method": admit that this situation / choice was a sub-optimal one... but perhaps in the long-run, it was a good thing that it happened, since it taught you X

For poor grades:There are all sorts of ways to phrase it, but state:
1) The reason for the bad grades in a matter-of-fact way.  See if you can stick in (SUBTLY!) a positive personality trait / or "good lesson learned" in there too, such as not giving up, or "this heavy involvement in extra-curriculars taught me a lot about managing others / managing my time":
- Previous schooling didn't prepare you / 1st Gen so didn't know things like "go to office hours" 
- Too involved in extra-curriculars (Silver lining could be learning about managing others; or making an impact on other students via this leadership) 
- Working to financially support your studies (Lesson about time management? Did that job teach you about, e.g. how to work with people from very different backgrounds, if it was a sort of manual labor job?)
- I was just an immature kid, goofing off: "Looking back, I lacked the maturity to take full advantage of the opportunites I had in college. Almost flunking out my sophomore year was a wake-up call that set me back on the right track..."  or  "Looking back, I simply lacked the maturity to take my studies seriously; thankfully, in the past 5 years I've spent in my career, I realize what a missed opportunity that was. I've recently embraced new ways to learn, for example, I enrolled in a course on AI from edX, where I got a top grade." 
 *Note: do NOT try to FORCE a "silver lining" if one truly can not be found. If it feels fake to you, it'll probably seem fake to a reader! 
2) Followed by the other evidence (of either academic ability, work-ethic, or both) that you have.
- GMAT / GRE is your best bet here, since it's a standardized test 
- Highlight doing well in quant classes, e.g. even if your overall GPA was not great, maybe in the "core" classes of interest to business schools such as calculus, statistics, etc. you did well? 
- If you did poorly in an introduction class, did you do *better* in a later version of that same subject? (e.g. "While I got a 'C' in 'Introduction to Organic Chemistry' my sophomore year, I later got "As" in the 2 follow-up courses, such as 'Advanced Organic Chemistry") 
- Does your current job involve quant / analytical work? If so, you can mention that as evidence that you will do well in their classroom.  
"Coming from an under-funded public high school with no AP science/math classes (very few science classes, period), adapting to the engineering curriculum at Cal was a bumpy ride, to say the least. While it was tempting to drop out and major in economics instead, I persevered and ultimately completed the BSE degree. I'm glad I stuck with it, since it taught me that I could tackle any challenge.
I'd respectfully ask that the Admissions Committee look at my GMAT score and my strong dedication to my job as proof that I will be able to thrive in the MBA academic environment. "

For low GMAT / GRE score:
Unfortunately, this situation is not as easy to explain, since it's a "standardized" test (ie, everyone faces roughly the same situation), and especially now that it's often possible to take the test at home (so excuses about lack of testing centers doesn't hold as much weight as it used to). 
Also, if you only took the test once, then they might be a little disappointed that you did not try to re-take it (even if that would have meant pushing to a later round).
- Provide evidence that the test score is not a full / accurate reflection of your academic capabilities: 
- For underperformance in quant, point to excellent grades in quant-heavy classes in college, and/or a quant-heav / analytical role or project at work.
- For underperformance in verbal, if you are a native English speaker:
- Highlight (if there was no other place to do so in the application form) how important good communication has been to your professional success (that is, that since your job requires such strong communication skills (e.g. "I regularly draft resarch repots that are sent out to clients, and effective writing is key") 
If you are not a native English speaker, you can also highlight: 
- Your TOEFL (or equivalent) score
- Work you do with foreign colleagues in English (e.g. "In my office, I am collaborating with colleauges in Norway and Israel on a daily basis, and English is the language we use in all of our communications")
"I do not believe that my GMAT score represents my true academic abilities, and would respectfully request that the admissions committee look to (my undergraduate performance in one of my nation’s most competitive colleges / my frequent promotions at a top-tier firm / whatever other proof that you’re just as smart as others ) as a more accurate reflection of my academic capabilities."
- "I'm just a bad test-taker!": then point to a previous time that a test under-estimated your academic capabilities (e.g. "For example, despite only scoring a 1200 on the SAT [250 points lower than the average score at my college], I still graduated with honors.") Do NOT say "I'm a bad test-taker!" but more like: "Standardized tests have not been a good predictor of my academic aptitude" 
-  "I'm a GOOD test taker, but that specific day was crazy, because something legitimately tragic occured!" Then you can go ahead and explain what it was (e.g. "Unfortunately a close family member passed away that morning, and I was not able to focus.")... while they will have empathy for that, please realize that they will probably ask something like: "Wow, that is a real shame... but then why didn't he re-take the test a few weeks later?" 
- In a desperate pinch, you can point to previous standardized test success: "I realize that the SAT is 'ancient history' for MBA application purposes, but for the SAT, I did score a X00 in math and a Y00 in verbal, which was 9Xth percentile, and helped me get accepted to an elite college."
- "I'm a GOOD test taker, but that specific day was crazy, though not in a serious way! My apartment flooded, the power went out, I was visited by a ghost, and I was having a bad hair day!": Careful to only explain truly bad things, vs. sounding like you're just making excuses / whining. 
- If something like "the power went out" really did happen, then chances are, that score would have been cancelled and a re-take would have been scheduled?  Or a grumpy response to that would have been: "Well, if your home does not have strong internet, then why did you take it there without any back-ups?" 
- As above, if you do have a very good test result on an earlier test, e.g. from high school, you can mention it here -- sure, it looks desperate, but.. well, you actually are desperate, so....? 
- "I have ADHD / crippling test anxiety / depression": I'd tread carefully here and probably not mention it. I think a cold-hearted response to that risks being: "Well ok, so then for the ADHD, why didn't you ask for an accomodation? There is a process for that, after all!"  or for a mental health challenge,  "Ok but if you know that this is an issue you're struggling with, why have you not yet sought out professional help / started on medication to try to help?" 
- "I am planning to re-take the test, to reach my full potential, but that will be after the admissions deadline": You can go ahead and mention this, but realize that they are under no obligation at all to consider that 2nd test score. The deadline exists for a reason, and they could easily respond that "Poor planning on your part does not constitute an emergency on our part.".  That is, if you signed up to take the test for the first time only a week before the deadlines, and did not do well... well, they might counter that: "Why would they cut it so close?" 
- Usually, they would say: "Well, if you think you can do better, then why not apply in another round / another year?" 
- But since that's sometimes not possible! You can say something like: 
"...While I realize that this will be after the submission deadline, I would like to share that I am currently scheduled to retake the test on February 7th, in which I expect to perform closer to my practice test scores of 750+. I did not push my application to next year since, with 6 years of work experience, I feel that next year will be the right time for me to enroll. I fully acknowledge that I am, of course, not officially entitled to have this new score considered; however, should you find the rest of my application competitive, I'd greatly appreciate the chance to send an updated score at that time." 
- Note: this argument will hold more weight if it's done in Round 2 / a late round / if you are on the older side (e.g. near the top-range of work experience for a given program)... if you are a younger applicant, then... why not simply wait a year and apply with a stronger score then? Why take a risk like this?

Unless otherwise asked for, I would NOT share what your (presumably bad) score is, nor provide explanations as to why your test score was bad. 
Instead, the advice here is nearly identical to the advice for poor grades (above): 
- Provide "Evidence" of your academic abilities / ability to handle rigorous business coursework (in particular, the quant coursework), via things like:
- Good grades in quant or quanty-business classes in college (calculus; statistics; finance) 
- If your current job requires analytical business skills, e.g. building financial models, analyzing balance sheets, operational optimizations, etc. 
- You could also even talk about *general* analytical skills your job requires (even though, admittedly, this isn't quite as relevant), e.g. if you're an engineer, but:
- Maybe mention a recent time you've had to teach yourself something new / quanty, to signal that you're confident that you'll be able to apply your current skill set to the new context of business (finance / operations)
- Be sure to avoid technical jargon (e.g. YES: "my role requires me to use calculus to analyze large data sets to send rockets into outer space, and last year I had to teach myself all about AI algorithms, so I am confident that I'll be able to adapt my analytical abilities to the business context"  and NOT "I routinely calculate the 1.21 gigawatts needed for the flux capacitor to...") 

For gaps in work experience, don't be afraid to be honest -- AdComs realize that the economy has been rough for several years.
The concern with gaps in work experience, and/or "only having a job for a short amount of time" is: does this perseon not work well with others? If one of our corporate recruiters hires them later on, will they end up firing this person too, thus bringing embarrassment to our school?" 
So, the main thing you need to prove is that you are an asset to employers, that they love you / value you as an employee (and thus, future employers won't have any issues with you at all!)
1. Explain the situation in a brief, matter-of-fact way:
- If you were laid-off, try to provide any evidence that "it wasn't just you", e.g. :
- "During COVID, 60% of my department was let go..." 
- "Our team had to restructure, and unfortunately they followed as "last in, first out" policy. Since I was the most junior person hired, I was the first to be let go. 
- If you have a recommender who can vouch for you here, that would also help a lot: "I was devastated when I had to lay her off, since she was such a strong performer; unfortunately, I did not have a choice." 
- If you were fired, then don't mention that (even if the reason for the firing was b.s. / your former boss was a psychopath, etc.) Just say: "After leaving Credit Suisse in Month, Year, I took time off and during that time I volunteered in my local community and..."  
- Note: do not *LIE* if you were fired (as it may eventually come up via a background check?) -- but what I'm saying is, don't pro-actively offer up that information 
- If you MUST explain it, can you make it about the culture not being a good fit? "The culture at McBain was much more cut-throat than I expected, and I did not perform well, and thus I was asked to leave".  
2. Also try to focus on:
- The positive things you did with / learned from this time:
- "This finally gave me a chance to reflect on what I really wanted, which is why I switched to company Y" 
- "I also used this time to volunteer in my community, which was very fulfililng." 
- Evidence from your career SINCE then that proves that you are trust-worthy, employable, get along well with others, are able to hold down a job. 
- "Thankfully, I then found my current group at CurrentEmployer, and for the past three years I'm lucky to have been able to work on interesting projects with great people." 
"Having worked so hard at Merrill Stanley for two years, I was devastated when 80% of my department (including all Analysts) were laid off. I used this time to re-evaluate what I wanted, and realized that I [did / did not] want to continue in banking. With this time, I learned how to seriously network for the first time. I also completed a yoga teacher certification, which has helped me tremendously with managing stress in subsequent years. While the lay-off wasn't fun, I learned a lot from it, which I think have made me a better person and leader."

For not using your boss as your recommender:
Just quickly explain:
1) Why you are not using them.
- If your real reason is "they hate me", then you can simply say that "They do not support my plans to pursue an MBA" (since... technically this is probably true? If they dislike you, then they probably don't support your plans, ha ha) 
- Another common reason is: "I have only been working for them for < 6 months", so I feel that they don't know me as well as this other person does..." 
2) ALSO why this other person is qualified to speak about you
- That is, since the purpose of asking for a current boss is to get a recent sense of your leadership style from someone who knows you well, has seen how to lead up-close, etc... then explain why this other person is *also* qualified to serve as a recommender
"My current boss does not support my plans for business school, so instead I'm submitting a recommendation from another VP in my department, with whom I have worked very closely for the past two years. She has seen me organize projects, work with others, and sell senior management on new ideas, so I feel that she can provide a strong perspective on my leadership style."
