In a hurry?
YOU MUST point to PAST EXAMPLES of you helping/contributing to others to PROVE to me that you really mean it! Otherwise, your promises will sound hollow. 
You must also point to a SPECIFIC BENEFIT your classmates will get from this contribution.
So: 
- Think about times you've contributed to a "community" (ANY community -- college, work (could be via an official activity at work, e.g. an employee support group, or even informally if you don't have much else to draw from), church/synagogue/temple... in a desperate pinch, maybe even a contribution you've made to the lives of friends and/or family) 
- For each time you've contributed something, think about "Ok, so how can I REPEAT / REPLICATE that commitment at Wharton?"
- Yes, a good answer here will require digging in to the different clubs' websites , getting familiar with the events / conferences /etc. that they offer, and picking a specific, concrete examples of something you will help with. 
- Then, the structure of your example(s) will be structured like this:
-[The thing you did in the past and what it accomplished]
-[The parallel contribution you can make at Wharton]
-  Note: of course you could swap the order and put your intended contribution first, and THEN back it up with evidence!  I have a sliiiiiiight preference for the "proof" first, just to prevent any moments of skepticism from even having a chance to enter their heads! 
-[If not obvious, explain why it will benefit your classmates]
Example:
"In college, I was student body president, where I worked closely with students to push through a major reform to our grading practices and introducing recycling to our dining hall.  At Wharton, I'd plan to reprise this role by running for the WGA, primarily the role of Academic Chair, to ensure that students' voices are heard by professors. I spoke with last year's Academic Chair, Jane Doe '20, who thought that my ideas around grading reform might help make the experience at Wharton even better than it already is."
^^^Note how this example brings up a specific community contribution this person made before, and then mentions a contribution to Wharton that is DIRECTLY and TIGHTLY tied in to the previous activity. 
- Then, if you have more than one thing to talk about, you'd repeat the above structure for each contribution. Put them in order of most impactful (e.g. anything that helps others' careers) to less impactful (helping with academics) to least impactful (something "fun", like a meditation club, or a wine club, or a... meditating while drinking wine club?)(maybe THAT's why I'm terrible at meditating? - IMPORTANT: QUALITY OVER QUANTITY.  2 or 3  STRONG contributions is waaaaay better than, say, 6 hurried / shallow ones
- Besides, you won't have time to be heavily active in six clubs (or whatever) anyway!

Avoid This Mistake!!!
Don't merely say, "....and I will bring this experience to help others in the Marketing Club. The end." Rather, bring your contribution to life with SPECIFIC THINGS you will do and SPECIFIC IDEAS you have for how to contribute.  
This will require doing some real research into Wharton's offerings... but would you expect a school this good to have a question that can be answered lazily? :) 
Look, CLOSELY, at what each club does. Picture yourself at a club meeting. Who are you? The Social Chair, organizing the next happy hour or party? The Treasurer, managing the club's finances? The VP of External Outreach, contacting companies for a "Trek" visit?  
If the club has an annual conference, look at recent and past conference agendas. Which panel or break-out topic would you be most able to lead, given your contacts? Which part of the conference would come most naturally to you -- managing the budget (since you already handled the budget of your sorority in college)? Handling the logistics and catering (since you already organized your company's offsite last year?)
What elevates a great answer here is something that paints a specific picture in the reader's head of WHICH things you will do, why, and HOW you will throw yourself into that activity. 
- NO: "I can't wait to help the Energy Club grow and reach its goals." (too vague! Not the full name of the club!) 
- YES: "...given my experience running the sound-board at my college's undergrad radio station, I'd love to be the audio engineer, optimizing the Energy & Climate Club's monthly podcast to sound its best."  (*Direct*, relevant tie in of previous activity to Wharton! Candidate did research and saw that the club has podcast! Candidate was THOUGHTFUL about how they could actually contribute. Candidate got the name of the club correct!) :) 
- Note: you only need to write the name of the club out in its full glory once... no need to use its full name if you reference it multiple times! 
- And yes, the example of "I worked for the college radio station --> contibution to Wharton club" used above falls under the umbrella of: "Candidate who doesn't have a ton of extra-curricular stuff to talk about... but they can try to make up for it by being THOUGHTFUL!"

The Different Ways You Can Contribute: 
So here’s a quick overview of the various ways you can help your classmates:
Professionally (job hunt) -- this will usually be via a professional club -- Can you help people trying to get into your field? Set them up with contacts? Help them ace the interview? Teach them how you raised money for your startup? If you’re a techie, can you help aspiring entrepreneurs judge the feasibility of their start-up’s idea?
Bonus points: if you’ve been involved at hiring in your firm, then you can definitely point to being able to help classmates prep for interviews.
Even if you are a career-changer, can you find a more indirect way to help the club? E.g. if you organize training sessions for new hires at work, can you bring those organization / teaching skills to the club (even if you're not a subject matter expert yet?) 
If you're aiming for one of the consulting / banking / product manager career paths, where the summer internship is usually the key to getting a full-time offer after graduation, then you could say something like:
"If I succeed in getting an internship in VC, then during my second year I can help other career-switchers like me with job interview prep" 
I don't LOVE this, since A LOT OF DOMINOES need to line up just right for this contribution to end up happening... but if you're grasping at straws, then... 
Professional Clubs & Conferences:  In Essay #1, we talked about what we’d LEARN from each club. Now in THIS essay, we’ll talk about what we’ll GIVE to each club/conference.  (The full list of ALL clubs is here: https://mba.wharton.upenn.edu/extracurricular-activities/ )
Note: sometimes the website links seem to be broken... you might need a little extra Google effort in this case to make sure the club still exists!) 
THE IMPORTANT THING FOR CLUBS IS TO NOT SIMPLY SAY THAT YOU WILL “JOIN” THEM, BUT RATHER THAT YOU WILL ACTIVELY CONTRIBUTE TO THEM.  So instead of “I’ll join the Whatever Club”, state that you plan to take a leadership role in that club, and ideally try to tie in that leadership position with something you’ve done in the past.
- If you already work in the industry: Say you can take a leadership role in the club and if applicable, will invite your boss or your contacts from your industry to speak (perhaps at the conference).
- If you don’t yet work in the industry: Acknowledge that in exchange for the benefits you’ll get, you’re looking forward to contributing too… try to tie this contribution to something in your background or something you’ve done in the past, like:  “I was the social chair for my sorority in college; I’d be happy to take on the role of [FIND A SIMILAR ROLE] at Wharton.”  Do you work in finance? See if the club has a budget you can help manage. Do you work in operations/project management? The conference team will LOVE your help. Are you a techie? Surely the club’s website could use some freshening up?

[Optional] My hypothesis as to why they changed the question a couple of years ago
So, this version of the question is a THROW BACK to the OLDER version of this question. 
Several years ago, the question was: 
"Teamwork is at the core of the Wharton MBA experience with each student contributing unique elements to our collaborative culture. How will you contribute to the Wharton community? (400 words maximum)"
THEN the question was: 
"Describe an [Maria's note: JUST ONE!] impactful experience or accomplishment that is not reflected elsewhere in your application. How will you use what you learned through that experience to contribute to the Wharton community? (400 words)"
And yet now, they've swung back to: 
"Taking into consideration your background – personal, professional, and/or academic – how do you plan to make specific, meaningful contributions to the Wharton community? (400 words maximum)"
 So. They went from asking a vague question about your "unique elements" that you'd contribute, to asking for JUST ONE example. 
This year, they've swung back to asking about multiple contributions AND they are PRETTY SPECIFIC about "personal, professional, academic" contributions... 
What this says to me is that:
- Earlier, they may have gotten a lot of really esoteric, airy-fairy contribution ideas. I suspect the earlier wording made people obsess too much over the word "unique", so I think where a lot of people went wrong was to try TOO HARD to be "zany" vs. keeping focused on the word "contribution" 
- In the past, they clearly did not feel that getting only ONE example was satisfying
- SO -- since they went back to MULTIPLE contributions, and they replaced the "unique" wording to way more standard "personal, professional, and/or academic"... they want at LEAST TWO contributions, and they want them to be CONCRETE (vs. my "unique elements", which is pretty vague and / or might invite some "wacky" answers)
- In other words, "unique elements" I think EITHER invited people to try too hard to be zany ("I can train other students' hamsters how to ride a unicycle!")  OR, it was soooo vague that it didn't actually lead to anything solidly concrete ("My contribution will be my effervescent, playful sense of style!" Um. Ok cool but... we're here for our... careers, after all?) 
Advice for dual-degree applicants (in general) and Lauder applicants (specifically)
In a hurry?
MOST dual degrees (mercifully) generally ask a "statement of purpose" type of question -- e.g. "Career Goals? Why this degree, why our program?", so the good news is, the work / rationale / explanation building muscles you've been strengthening in the Lab will work here too...(just with different classes, etc., cited)
...you just need to balance between a career goal that TRULY REQUIRES both degrees (vs. "Well, why get a JD when you could just hire a lawyer later for a lot less money?") and also balance your ENTHUSIASM for and REFERENCES TO the academics / extra-curriculars / internship opportunities/  etc. between the 2 programs -- no lop-sided enthusiasm (TRUST ME: IT SHOWS)
Obviously, since the 2 applications will probably be read together, the career vision "hook" must be THE SAME in both essays (in terms of substance, NOT a copy-and-paste!), but you can obvious focus a bit more on the business side of things in the MBA essay and the (other degree) side of things in the (other degree) essay. 
As always, mention:
- Specific classes + a plausible future scenario where you'd really be putting that class' lessons to work 
- One sort of "Experiential" activity (if applicable) and not only "Hey, I want to go to Israel" but tell me WHY *that* particular trek / trip / project is useful for you
- At least one "club" or non-academic activity you plan to join, as well as what you can contribute to your fellow classmates.

The Lauder SOP essay  
The full text of the question: 
Discuss why you are applying to the Lauder Institute, detailing specific reasons, experiences, and background for choosing your Program of Concentration, as well as your interest in further study in that Program.
Applicants to Programs of Concentration involving language study (Africa-French; East and Southeast Asia; Europe; Latin America; South Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa) should also discuss what they hope to gain from advanced language and cultural education, as well as describe how they have learned the language they will study (e.g. undergraduate courses, undergraduate major, etc.). Finally, please describe how you see yourself benefiting from, and contributing to, the Lauder community.   (800 words)
-^^^ THIS WORD COUNT USED TO BE "between 400 - 500 words"! By generously extending it so much, they've made it MUCH easier (in terms of editing energy)... but it also tells us that they are looking for SPECIFICS!
Look, essentially this essay is asking the EXACT SAME STUFF as the other Wharton essays, except it's in one big essay (vs. breaking out the "get" and the "give" into 2), and also the word count is identical. 
If you've already done the Wharton essays, then it will be the SAME CORE INGREDIENTS (with the career hook worded differently of course!) -- mentioning classes, hands-on experiences (e.g. the travel-based experiences), and what specific gap / which specific future hypothetical situation each offering will fill for you, en route to your career goal. 
I *think* that you will NOT talk about doing a global program like GIP in the Wharton essay, since you'll get a ton of travel opps with Lauder, and so the point wil have been made there (ask current students though, if they end up fitting in both?) -- I mean, LOOK AT ALL OF THE IMMERSIONS THEY OFFER? Presumably you'd do most of your "serious" (vs a fun vacation) travel with them? https://lauder.wharton.upenn.edu/immersion/
Similarly, regarding clubs, since the Lauder program is relatively small (about 80 people) and is made up of both MBAs and JDs, the impression I have is that there aren't really any "clubs" per se that are only for Lauder -- so your "Contribution" is going to be your experiences that will be relevant for your classmates:  professional, cultural, regulatory, governmental, etc. to help THEM learn how to do business in the region of focus.
Key elements for this essay:
-Career Vision – must be the SAME* as in the Wharton essay, since they will be read together
-* worded differently, of course! You can also focus more on the "regional" aspect of the career vision more here, vs. perhaps the industry / functional aspect for Wharton
-If NOT OBVIOUS, you MAY want to spend some time here explaining not only the “origin story” for the career interest, but also the “original story” for “WHY THIS REGION?”
-(Obviously if you were, say, born in a given region, but then moved someplace else as a child or something, then you may not need that much elaboration)
-Which of the six concentrations you’ll choose (though let's be honest -- this should be obvious from the career vision, n'est-ce pas?) 
- If you're a true multi-region dynamo, then pick the broader "Global" concentration
-Which language you’ll focus in (and what you hope to gain in the language)
- e.g. lots of us might know academic French, or how our family speaks Japanese at home... but the business world (and legal, regulatory, etc.) has its own vocabulary and nuance, so if you speak a language from previous study / learning it at home, but have not worked extensively in the region, then you could say that you want to focus on learning "business German".
-Classes: which of the classes (e.g. from the Summer Immersion) will be MOST relevant for your future career goal
-Plus the plausible hypothetical future scenario where you will use that class pragmatically (this is the same for Wharton + other "Why MBA" essays too!!!)
- Sample list of Summer Immersion classes can be found here: https://lauder.wharton.upenn.edu/immersion/first-summer-immersion/
-Which of the current Lauder International Ventures (LIVS) will you do? (you do two of them)  -- they DON’T have to be in your “region” per se, but they should ideally tie in to something related to your field of interest (don’t “marry” one, since it might not be offered later on, or you might not get chosen for one)
- Link to current LIVS is here: https://lauder.wharton.upenn.edu/immersion/livs/

-This MUST be in your region of interest (or if you’re “global”, then involve multiple regions) AND involve knowledge of mastery of your target language. It is MORE “academic” / research focused vs. hands-on MBA paper

They *seem* to have removed this entirely, but sometimes schools move things to tricky places in an application form that I don't stumble upon, so if you DO find that question, LMK and I'll re-add in my advice (though honestly, it wasn't anything mind-blowing and by this point, you can probably read my mind a bit as to what I'll say about THIS! 
I suppose one pitfall would be if a candidate tries to make their thesis topic sound suuuuuuper grandiose but therefore unrealistic in the process; e.g. "I'll write my thesis about how supporting women-led SMEs will bring peace to the middle east once and for all!".  This risks sounding immature, or like a mis-guided attempt to try to impress them. Or if the thesis veers too far into "MPP"/ public policy territory? 
Being able to say something like: "I spoke with Pierre LaPierre, who is studying French, and he said that a thesis from the previous year explored Blah blah blah in Morocco... I'd love to do something similar for Brazil!" will not only help you wrap your head around a good potential topic, but also will show that you did super strong research and therefore are taking the program pretty seriously!

(Optional) All reapplicants to Wharton are required to complete the Optional Essay. Explain how you have reflected on the previous decision about your application, and discuss any updates to your candidacy (e.g., changes in your professional life, additional coursework, extracurricular/volunteer engagements (Limit: 250 words)


(This advice will be pretty much the same from school-to-school, though there's a bit of Wharton-specific advice here, given how they've phrased the question)
Their “explain how you have reflected" on getting dinged last year is an interesting twist. Be mature here, maybe even saying something like, “I now realize that perhaps my leadership experience was not yet robust enough for me to contribute to my classmates’ experience in the best way. NOW, however….[explain how this has improved]" or “In retrospect, my career vision may not have been fully thought-out. NOW, however…[explain how this has improved].
Also, Wharton’s word-count of 250 isn’t exactly a ton of room, so if you have an AMAZING accomplishment from the past year, consider mentioning it in the main essay too.
 
I know you already know this, but don’t mention a petty / minor reason, like, “Well, I messed up my interview last year, so this time around, I know that I should prepare more". Don’t say anything that implies to them that they somehow made a mistake, or that you WERE truly qualified, but you simply messed up something like that GMAT.
 
The main thing to mention here isn’t just “Well, in the past year I’ve gotten to work on more stuff, and here is a list of that stuff". Rather, be sure to call out what you’ve learned from these additional experiences:
"In the past year, I've asked my boss to please include me more on group projects. This is what allowed me to work on the XYZ project, which was also my first time leading a multi-national team. It also gave me more of an opportunity to see the strategic "big picture" of our industry, which I didn’t have last year."
Also, consider directly addressing how you’ll now benefit your classmates more, based on these new experiences:
"With this new experience to draw from, I’m looking forward to helping my classmates in X."
 
For example, “As an added benefit, my strengthened relationship with our company’s recruiting efforts will allow me to help my future classmates prep for on-campus interviews!."
Optional Essay: Please use this space to share any additional information about yourself that cannot be found elsewhere in your application and that you would like to share with the Admissions Committee. This space can also be used to address any extenuating circumstances (e.g., unexplained gaps in work experience, choice of recommenders, inconsistent or questionable academic performance, areas of weakness, etc.) that you would like the Admissions Committee to consider. (500 words)


"Extenuating Circumstances" is diplomatic AdCom speak for "something you either messed up or is a little weird in your application". In other words, this is the typical "optional' essay for explaining low GPAs, why your current boss is not a recommender, why you only held that job for x months, why you were unemployed for y months, etc.
[The advice for the optional essay is basically the same for all schools, so if you've read this for another school, it will be the same here!]

First of all, if you did have a major life-changing event that led to poor academics / periods of unemployment, consider weaving it into a main essay story, to address it head-on. If you do that, then you can potentially leave the optional essay blank. However, many schools have "Career Vision / Why MBA?" types of essays only, so in that case, there's a strong chance you'll need to write an optional essay too.
Keep all optional essays:

Brief

Factual

Not whiny ("poor me / boo hoo!")

Not too apologetic ("I totally suck! You'll probably reject me!")

YES: MATURE. If you made a mistake, own up to it and what you learned from it. :)
If possible, put a “silver lining" spin on things -- yes, maybe that 20-hr-per-week job meant that your grades were low, but on the plus side, it taught you how to manage others, so net-net, it was a good thing for you!
For not using your boss as your recommender: Just quickly explain why, and ALSO why this other person is also qualified to speak about you.

"My current boss does not support my plans for business school, so instead I'm submitting a recommendation from another VP in my department, with whom I have worked very closely for the past two years. She has seen me organize projects, work with others, and sell senior management on new ideas, so I feel that she can provide a strong perspective on my leadership style."

For poor grades:There are all sorts of ways to phrase it, but just state the reason for the bad grades in a matter-of-fact way, followed by the other evidence (of either academic ability, work-ethic, or both) that you have. See if you can stick in (SUBTLY!) a positive personality trait / or "good lesson learned" in there too, such as not giving up:

"Coming from an under-funded public high school with no AP science/math classes (very few science classes, period), adapting to the engineering curriculum at Cal was a bumpy ride, to say the least. While it was tempting to drop out and major in economics instead, I persevered and ultimately completed the BSE degree. I'm glad I stuck with it, since it taught me that I could tackle any challenge. I'd respectfully ask that the Admissions Committee look at my GMAT score and my strong dedication to my job as proof that I will be able to thrive in the MBA academic environment. "

For low GMAT score (especially quant): If you had a bad day / something traumatic happen prior to the test, or if you simply don’t test well, then point to other “evidence" that you can compete at the same level of your future peers :

"I do not believe that my GMAT score represents my true academic abilities, and would respectfully request that the admissions committee look to (my undergraduate performance in one of my nation’s most competitive colleges / my frequent promotions at a top-tier firm / whatever other proof that you’re just as smart as others ) as a more accurate reflection of my academic capabilities."

For gaps in work experience, don't be afraid to be honest -- AdComs realize that the economy has been rough for several years. Explain the situation in a brief, matter-of-fact way. If you were laid-off, try to provide any evidence that "it wasn't just you". Also try to focus on the positive things you did with / learned from this time:

"Having worked so hard at Merrill Stanley for two years, I was devastated when 80% of my department (including all Analysts) were laid off. I used this time to re-evaluate what I wanted, and realized that I [did / did not] want to continue in banking. With this time, I learned how to seriously network for the first time. I also completed a yoga teacher certification, which has helped me tremendously with managing stress in subsequent years. While the lay-off wasn't fun, I learned a lot from it, which I think have made me a better person and leader."
