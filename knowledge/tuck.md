A note about the Tuck "Open Interview Policy" 
 Previously, Tuck had an "open interview policy" where, for a few weeks each year, anyone who could get themselves up to Hanover was guaranteed an interview. 
Now, they have changed that to: anyone who submits their full application by September 1 will be guaranteed an interview (or October 1 / Dec 1 if you're applying via the Consortium): 
https://www.tuck.dartmouth.edu/admissions/visit-interview
Even if you can’t make it to campus or submit before Sept 1, reach out to them ASAP to be connected with a current student. They actually are thoughtful about who they connect you with, so this is a great and relatively unique resource. As their AdCom said during my visit, "We are looking for people who have gone out of their way to get to know Tuckies in their natural habitat": https://www.tuck.dartmouth.edu/admissions/ambassadors

Which brand traits is Tuck looking for?
According to Tuck's own admissions criteria, the four personality traits they are looking for in candidates are: 
Smart (and now also "curious" and "engaged" have been added):
Do not try to prove this via the essays; this is proven via the GMAT + your transcripts 
The one exception here would be telling a story where you either "learn from or teach others" -- that is, you're curious enough to try to learn new things from other people
That is, you take an active role in trying to learn things vs. simply sitting back and waiting for someone to teach you
Nice has been replaced by: "encouraging, collaborative, and empathetic"
Why the change from the long-standing "nice"? 
I strongly suspect that they got lots of applications of people, say, thinking nice thoughts, but then NOT ACTUALLY DOING ANYTHING to be "nice" via their ACTIONS.  
You SHOULD for sure prove this via the stories both you and your recommenders tell
In an admissions webinar, they said multiple times that this can be "investing in the success of others"
Accomplished (and now also "impactful" and "principled"):
Another trap! Do NOT use the essays / recommendations to only focus on what a high-achiever you are (it risks sounding arrogant, which is the opposite of "nice") -- instead, the resume is where your accomplishments will go; your recommenders will sing the praises of your impact on your organization
Again, notice the addition of the word "impactful" is a clear desperate cry for CONCRETE ACTIONS / RESULTS vs some sort of "accomplishment' that is irrelevant, like winning a piano competition
Note also the addition of "principled" means -- no, we do NOT want a "win at all costs" type. You need accomplishments, but you need to have achieved them in a noble, honorable way. 
Aware (and now also "ambitious" and "purposeful"):
They said: "Reflection is mandatory at Tuck. You need to understand your values, assess your experiences, and articulate how they’ve shaped your identity and character. You also need to demonstrate the capacity to receive and learn from direct feedback."
They also mention how "aware" also ties in to career goals; presumably they mean realistic career goals vs. "pie in the sky" goals
As they put it: "coherent goals, audacious in scope yet grounded in reality" 
This school has been very open about how closely their Career Center and Admissions Office work together re: "How realistic is this person's career vision?"

Not a brand trait per se, but demonstrating awareness of how your past + Tuck can help you reach your ambitious (but still realistic) goal. 
There is one more Lab brand trait that matters for Tuck, even though it's not officially one of their 4 core criteria, and that brand trait is:
Global
A few years ago, Tuck did a strategic analysis on itself and decided that one area where they were less competitive than schools in larger cities was in the international sphere. 
(makes sense -- this is in large part because they are about a 3 hour drive away from the closest large airport!) 
As part of this, they not only strengthened what their global offerings are in order to compete with other schools, but they are also looking for students who can bring that global perspective to Tuck.

HIDDEN mini-essays in the application form: Share your short term goals. Share your long term goals. (300 characters each)
So, in the application form itself, there are 2 text boxes with a word-count of 50 words each to explain your short-term and then your long-term career goals. 
So, you need to balance parts of your career story BETWEEN these 2 boxes and the longer, more traditional "Why Tuck?" essay. 
My advice? Try to write the full essay #1 first. Then, see what you need to pull out from that in order to make room for the word-count limit. 
If you're able to put (long-term hook + short-term job [+" homework" that it's feasible] + "Why Tuck") into 300 words, then in these boxes, I would keep it as brief as possible. 
However, if you need more room to elaborate (career switchers, I'm looking at you) then you can use these mini-responses strategically to fill in the gaps that there simply is not any room for in the main essay #1.
Update: in mid-2020, their head of AdComs wrote this blog post, which, to me, has these most valuable lines:
Both your short- and long-term goals should be ambitious and realistic, though on the margin, we will expect your long-term goals to tilt towards the former and your short-term goals to tilt towards the latter.
...we’re assessing that your goals are coherent, ambitious, and purposeful.
More description on how to approach this is here:
Approach #1: 
If you think that the overall narrative will flow better if you go from (long-term hook) --> (short term goal) --> (why Tuck MBA?) all in one place (essay #1), then in THESE boxes you can simply state your goal in very few words (This is my preferred approach): 
- "My short-term goal is to pivot to a job in CPG brand management, ideally in the food industry"
- "My long-term goal is to help organic food companies grow by becoming a CMO"
Note that in the blog post linked above, Luke said that you can also list a "personal goal" here if you really, really want tod, but I think for most people, that will come across as lame:
"My post-graduation goals are to build upon my financial modeling skills to get a job in investment banking, and also, to run a marathon" (???)
Approach #2:
You can move some more substantial content that would have otherwise gone into the larger essay here. 
This could include some or all of:
- Your "hook" (what is the Big Problem you want to solve, and why?) -- this would go in the long-term answer.
- Which current skills do you have that will be applicable / attractive to your future employers (this is particularly important for anyone making a career switch) (this will go in the short-term answer) - this is primarily important for career-switchers
- The "short-term career realism homework" that you might have done that you truly can reach your short-term goal, for example interviewing people who have the job you want who have endorsed your background for the job. (same as above -- can be put in short-term answer and is primarily necessary for career switchers)
- What the benefit will be from the short-term job that will help you reach / shine in the long-term goal 
The benefit of this approach is that it frees you up to include a lot more "Why Tuck" into the "Why Tuck" essay. 
The draw-back is that now what should otherwise be a story that flows nicely, things will now be more disjointed. They will PROBABLY read the resume + this part near the beginning of the application review...and then they'll get to the full essays a bit later. 
If you move a LOT of your content into these 100 (total) words, do remind them of the goals in the essay (don't assume that they'll magically memorize it, even if they only read these blurbs a few minutes ago):
- "Tuck -- both its offerings and its community -- is the perfect place for me to one day become a leader in the organic foods industry." 
And then the 50 words each will look like: 
Short-term:
- "My post-MBA career goal is to work in brand management for a CPG company, perferably one that has a food division. I've already developed a strong background in digital marketing (advertising; social media), but working as a brand manager will broaden the scope of my expertise." 
or: 
- "My post-MBA career goal is to work in brand management for a CPG company, perferably one that has a food division. I spoke with Tuck alumna Jane Smith ('18), who said that my digital marketing background is a solid foundation to make this career switch." 
Legend:
= Evidence that I can realistically get this job!
= Why this short-term job is useful for my long-term path
= Proof that I made an effort to connect with Tuckies
Long-term:
- "I was saddened when my aunt was diagnosed with lymphoma; I was angry when I read that it may have been caused by pesticides in wheat-based products like bread and cereal. My long-term career mission is to help organic food companies grow, perhaps as a CMO."
Legend:
= Moving the hook here, to free up space [about 30 words] in essay 1
= Focusing on the long-term impact / mission I have for my career, vs. obsessing on reaching a specific title / level. 
= Ambitious (but also showing flexibilty in the path)

A note on how career realism is especially important to Tuck
A few years ago, I attended an event for admissions consultants hosted by Tuck's AdCom. Here are a few key takeaways regarding the career vision:
They DO ask the Career Development Office if your career goal is realistic
"Is this plan realistic?"
If it's a stretch, do they also explain a possible "Plan B"?
We DON'T want people at the end of the two years to be upset that their career goals did not come true
"Don't just tell us the career story; tell us why those steps make sense."
"Does this mean that I can't apply with a career switching story?" 
Actually, I'm more open to it with Tuck vs. other schools, given its alumni network...
...as someone on their AdCom said: "...or if there are gaps [between what their current career is vs. their future career], does our program / curriculum support making that transition?" 
So, if you ARE going to apply with a career-switching story, just make sure it's jumping into a field that Tuck has particular strength in

Q: Tell us who you are. How have your values and experiences shaped your identity and character? How will your unique background contribute to Tuck and/or enhance the experience of your classmates? (2000 characters)
In a hurry?
Your "individuality" is asking for how you're unique, in a sea of nearly-identical applicants -- but DO NOT PANIC. You don't need to be an Olympic Astronaut Ballerina here. 
What can be discussed here are: 
- Ways you're unique amongst others in your applicant cohort (that is, the other people with similar resumes who are also applying) 
- e.g. "One professional experience I've had that is relatively unique was having to 
- Any outside passions you have that you think stand out 
- The passion or hobby itself might not be that unique, but you can show your "indviduality" by approaching the hobby or getting benefits from the hobby in a way that you can put your own individual spin on ?
- Your personality -- it can be interwoven within the overall essay, or if you've got some evidence / a story that shows it, then elaborate on it here
- This could even be some key person, experience who has shaped who you are...or a value you hold dear that you feel sets you apart and makes you "Tucky"
THEN, if you can, TIE IN how at least one of these experiences, values,  and/or expertise will help your classmates who are woven alongside you in the "fabric of Tuck":
- In class? Via the "professional" clubs (helping them get jobs)? Via the non-professional clubs (and thus making the campus a more fun / welcoming place to be?  Your personality?
 HOWEVER, unlike in previous years (read about the previous essay prompt below) you do NOT HAVE TO *FORCE* this unique thing about you into a Tuck-related offering. If it flows naturally and organically - great! If not, no need to stress about it. 

 
"What if something that makes me 'unique' does NOT directly map to a Tuck offering, like a club?"
Just because it does not tie in to a Tuck offering, does not mean that it can be something that violates Tuck's values. It needs to, at a minimum, implicitly prove that you're culturally a "Tuckie".
Yes, every thread in the fabric is an individual, unique thread, BUT the shared values of Tuck are what bind the individual threads together into a cohesive fabric (community)!
So, if something unique about you is that you volunteer with animal shelters, even if it doesn't tie in to a specific campus contribution, I think you can totally write about it, since it shows (even indirectly) compassion and generosity of spirit. 
On the other hand, if something unique about you is that you won a math competition or are a member of a "genuis" society like MENSA... well, how does that show that you're nice? What does it say about your value system, that you see this prompt and immediately think to brag about how smart / "accomplished" you are? What does that say about your maturity? 
(Yes, I know that "smart" is one of their criteria, but THAT is proven by GMAT/grades; please don't address how "smart" you are in an essay. It will feel gross.) 
 "What if something that makes me unique actually *IS* a solitary achievement or award?"
 For an objective achievement: 
Let's say something that you feel makes you unique is that you are a triathlete. Being a triathlete is super-impressive, BUT it does not automatically mean that someone is nice, compassionate, giving, etc. In fact, some might worry that a competitive athlete might be too obsessed with "winning at all costs" and might end up being a competitive jerk on campus! 
In a situation like this, YES, of COURSE you can mention "winning a triathlon" as something that is unique to you. 
BUT. 
Challenge yourself to tie it in to SOME sort of "EQ" trait. Have you used your sport to raise money for charity? Have you helped train inner-city kids in your sport? Does training for your sport relieve stress, clear your head, etc. such that you are a nicer person at work? 
In this case, save the "achievement" for the resume (e.g. winning a big race) and instead in the essay write about how this solitary endeavor affects you on an (inside) emotional level, and not an (outside) external accolade. 
For an award or acknowledgement of some sort:
Tread lightly. The chances of pulling this off withOUT seeming like an immature braggart are slim!
Look at how off-putting these sample statements are:
"In college, I won the 'best delegate' award in the nation-wide Model U.N. competition, granted to the attendee who made the best speech about global policy."
"I am unique in that I am the only engineer who gets to engage directly with clients -- even in high-level discussions such as contract negotiations. My skill in this earned me an early promotion and the 'Star Contributor award at work." 
"In high school, I scored in the top 0.1% in mathetmatics in my country's national university exam."
Even if a very nice person wrote the above, what does it say that they chose this external accolade as a chance to brag about their skills? 
Instead then, leave the awards for the resume, "awards" section of the application, or have your recommender mention it (if it was at work). 
If you feel you must write about this, instead I'd challenge you to write about the personal qualities, origin, evolution,  or significance of the broader skill that LED to the award. And /or, if you can point to an "EQ" benefit to this value, so much the better. This will not only prevent bragging but will come across as more personal.
Let's revisit the examples above: 
"I spent my childhood summers visiting my cousins in Peru. This helped me grow up seeing international issues from a more global, less U.S.-centric perspective. This has helped me in my subsequent career as I managed our outsourced engineers in Argentina."
 "Growing up as one of six children, I had to learn at an early age to consider others' points of view when communicating. For example, when negotiating who would get to spend time on the family computer, I tried to think about what non-computer activity I could offer my siblings instead. These communication skills have been surprisingly useful in my career, when negotiating with clients."
"In high school, I tutored my classmates in mathematics. I found that needing to formulate explanations to their questions not only helped them, but also helped me cement my understanding of the material. Plus, helping others felt great.  [note the indirect nod to doing well on a math test; the test itself might be mentioned elsewhere in the app] Today at work, I explain high-frequency trading concepts to our new Analysts, since getting them onboard quickly benefits the entire firm." 
"What if the thing I want to talk about is kind of weird? Is that risky or will my boldness be rewarded?"
Yes yes yes, adcoms say things like: "Be authentic! We want to get to know the real you!"...but that's not an excuse to let your freak flag fly!
So, I think there's "good" weird and "bad" weird.
"Good" weird examples:
- A delightful or endearing hobby - ideally one that in some way "gives back", but even if it doesn't, it might demonstrate something cute about you, like bird-watching or mastering yo-yo tricks.
 - Don't make something like this the MAIN THING you talk about though, unless it gives SOME insight into your personality, values, etc. so that I can picture you as a potential classmate in my teeny community
- Approaching the essay in an esoteric way, such as talking about your key values, how they were formed, and how you've lived them... or separating it out with each paragraph devoted to a city you've lived in? Or a person who has influenced you?
- As long as you keep in mind the goal of trying to picture "who you will be at Tuck", then there are lots of creative ways to choose what you write about. 
- Heck, as long as it answers the question, maybe someone who is a poet would want to reply in a poem format (?)
"Bad" weird examples:
- A childish, embarassing, or off-putting hobby ("Check out my toenail collection!") 
- Anything that might make the reader uncomfortable, such as a childhood trauma (unless today you volunteer or work as a counselor for other survivors of that trauma!) 
- Anything that would make them question your integrity ("I was arrested for shop-lifting in the 3rd grade!") 
- Anything that makes you seem NOT nice, NOT giving, NOT supportive of others (e.g. something selfish, braggy, feeling smug or "like a winner!", etc.)

A sample essay (and why I think it works)
Whenever a school issues a tricky essay like this, I pretend that I own a time machine and that I am going back in time to when *I* was an applicant -- how would *I* answer this question? 
The previous version of the question asked for direct contributions that our life experiences would have at Tuck. 
The new version is far more open-ended, and is about trying to figure out who you are.
Here's my stab at it (exactly 300 words) -- below, I'll explain my thought process behind each paragraph 
From Argentina to India, from Chile to China, I’ve had the opportunity to live and work in some of the most exciting countries on earth. My wanderlust was planted young: as a kid, I’d visit my mom’s family in Colombia, South America, every year. This was in the 1980’s, at the height of the “drug war” when Colombia (and its cocaine) was seen in every U.S. household as public enemy number one. Spending my summers in what was supposed to be an evil, dangerous place taught me that there are two sides to every story (after all, Colombia’s supply of cocaine would have nowhere to go if U.S. citizens weren’t the ones craving it!). This has translated in adulthood into being able to see multiple sides to every story, which has helped me adapt to the new cultures I’ve worked in.
Another part of who I am is someone with  a commitment to fighting on behalf of victims of abuse. In college, I spent a summer volunteering at Baltimore’s largest domestic violence clinic. There, I learned about the “cycle of abuse” that begins in childhood.  Realizing that I wanted to have an impact earlier in the cycle, when I lived in Miami I was a court-appointed volunteer for children in the foster care system. I plan to be devoted to this cause, even if in a small way, for the rest of my life.
Finally, if you were to ask both friends and colleagues about who I am, I think they’d mention my sense of humor. In college, I wrote for the humor magazine, and I’d love to help with the Follies at Tuck. Not only do I love entertaining others, I find that a humorous outlook on life helps me deal with the inevitable crises that come my way.
Thought process:
- Paragraph 1: 
- "What is unique?" - global work assignments; uncommon childhood vacations
- "How does it contribute to the fabric of Tuck?" - ability to understand many peoples' opinions, which hopefully means she'll be constructive and non-confrontational during class discussions. Also, international perspective could be useful in global classes or the GIX.
- Paragraph 2:
- "What is unique?" - while it feels like everyone has tutored an under-privileged child  at some point, this example is a more unique cause to be committed to. 
- "How does it contribute to the fabric of Tuck?" - this is someone who has shown sustained commitment to one cause over time, so it feels more genuine than people who only do things short-term. She has a "give back" value system, which we like here. 
- Paragraph 3:
- "What is unique?" - particular aspect of her personality (note that there is concrete evidence -- via a college activity -- that this is indeed who I am). Simply stating: "Oh, btw, I totally have personality trait X!" without any sort of "evidence" will fall flat. 
- "How does it contribute to the fabric of Tuck?" -- Well, she  does seem like a great candidate to write the Follies. At the very least, it demonstrates EQ skill of coping with stressors by taking a light-hearted view of them.

The key to a great optional essay is one that focuses on the positive -- the lesson learned -- from the event in question. :)
You can go back to the advice in the "Strengths and Weaknesses" module, but as a refresher, here are some ways to deal with common occurrences:
 
For not using your boss as your recommender: Just quickly explain why, and ALSO why this other person is also qualified to speak about you.
"My current boss does not support my plans for business school, so instead I'm submitting a recommendation from another VP in my department, with whom I have worked very closely for the past two years. She has seen me organize projects, work with others, and sell senior management on new ideas, so I feel that she can provide a strong perspective on my leadership style."
 
For poor grades:There are all sorts of ways to phrase it, but just state the reason for the bad grades in a matter-of-fact way, followed by the other evidence (of either academic ability, work-ethic, or both) that you have. See if you can stick in (SUBTLY!) a positive personality trait / or "good lesson learned" in there too, such as not giving up:
"Coming from an under-funded public high school with no AP science/math classes (very few science classes, period), adapting to the engineering curriculum at Cal was a bumpy ride, to say the least. While it was tempting to drop out and major in economics instead, I persevered and ultimately completed the BSE degree. I'm glad I stuck with it, since it taught me that I could tackle any challenge. I'd respectfully ask that the Admissions Committee look at my GMAT score and my strong dedication to my job as proof that I will be able to thrive in the MBA academic environment. "
 
For low GMAT score (especially quant): If you had a bad day / something traumatic happen prior to the test, or if you simply don’t test well, then point to other “evidence” that you can compete at the same level of your future peers :
"I do not believe that my GMAT score represents my true academic abilities, and would respectfully request that the admissions committee look to (my undergraduate performance in one of my nation’s most competitive colleges / my frequent promotions at a top-tier firm / whatever other proof that you’re just as smart as others ) as a more accurate reflection of my academic capabilities." or maybe something like:  "Thankfully, the rigors of my job don't occur in a timed, multiple-choice environment -- however, my job is extremely quantitative, and I have excelled in it, so I would respectfully ask that you consider my work history as evidence that I will excel in your courses."
 
For gaps in work experience, don't be afraid to be honest -- AdComs realize that the economy has been rough for several years. Explain the situation in a brief, matter-of-fact way. If you were laid-off, try to provide any evidence that "it wasn't just you". Also try to focus on the positive things you did with / learned from this time:
"Having worked so hard at Merrill Stanley for two years, I was devastated when 80% of my department (including all Analysts) were laid off. I used this time to re-evaluate what I wanted, and realized that I [did / did not] want to continue in banking. With this time, I learned how to seriously network for the first time. I also completed a yoga teacher certification, which has helped me tremendously with managing stress in subsequent years. While the lay-off wasn't fun, I learned a lot from it, which I think have made me a better person and leader."

(note: the advice for re-applicants is pretty much the same from school to school):
The key things to address in a re-application essay are:
How you’ve improved since the last time you applied:
New projects, new experiences, etc.
Coursework taken to improve your academic chops?
The main thing to mention here isn’t just “Well, in the past year I’ve gotten to work on more stuff, and here is a list of that stuff”. Rather, be sure to call out what you’ve learned from these additional experiences -- how will they make you a better student and contributor?:
"In the past year, I've asked my boss to please include me more on group projects. This is what allowed me to work on the XYZ project, which was also my first time leading a multi-national team. It also gave me more of an opportunity to see the strategic "big picture" of our industry, which I didn’t have last year."
If you think you were dinged before because your career vision was too weak, which steps have you taken (e.g., more informational interviews) to strengthen that vision and make it more attainable? Perhaps you have refined it and now truly appreciate how the MBA will get you there. 
Also, consider directly addressing how you’ll now benefit your classmates more, based on these new experiences:
"With this new experience to draw from, I’m looking forward to helping my classmates in X."
 “As an added benefit, my strengthened relationship with our company’s recruiting efforts will allow me to help my future classmates prep for on-campus interviews!.”
